\sect{1. Soundness is a question about one ideal}
A circuit over $\F_p$ is a list of polynomials $C=\{f_1,\dots,f_m\}\subset\F_p[x,\wb]$ in the public witness $x$ and the extended
witness $\wb$: the secret witness $w$ plus intermediates and hints. The accepted assignments are the $\F_p$-rational points of
$V(f_1,\dots,f_m)$. Soundness says every accepted assignment satisfies the specification $R$, and the solver refutes its negation
(slide 39):
\[
\forall(\wb,x):\ C(\wb,x)=0\ \Rightarrow\ R(w,x)
\qquad\text{fails iff}\qquad
\exists(\wb,x)\in\F_p^{\,n}:\ C(\wb,x)=0\ \wedge\ \neg R(w,x).
\]
Encode $\neg R$ as polynomials in fresh variables $t$ (\S2) and the whole query is one ideal
\[
\boxed{\;I=\bigl\langle f_1,\dots,f_m,\ \text{encoding of }\neg R\bigr\rangle\ \subset\ \F_p[x,\wb,t]\;}
\]
The circuit is sound exactly when $V(I)$ has no point with all coordinates in $\F_p$. The algebraic test is whether $1\in I$ (\S3).

\sect{2. Encoding $\neg R$}
A specification is a Boolean combination of polynomial equalities, and over a field each connective has an exact polynomial form:
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}\toprule
Logic & Polynomial & Why it is exact over a field\\\midrule
$g=0\ \wedge\ h=0$ & generators $g$ and $h$ & sum of ideals\\
$g=0\ \vee\ h=0$ & $g\,h$ & no zero divisors\\
$g\neq0$ & $t\,g-1$ & \textbf{Rabinowitsch}: $t=g^{-1}$ exists iff $g\neq0$ (localisation at $g$)\\
$g\neq0\ \wedge\ h\neq0$ & $t\,gh-1$ & a product of nonzero elements is nonzero\\
$g\neq0\ \vee\ h\neq0$ & $t_1g+t_2h-1$ & solvable iff $(g,h)\neq(0,0)$: take $t_1=g^{-1},\,t_2=0$ or vice versa\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
Two cases cover most circuits. If $R$ is a conjunction $g_1=\dots=g_k=0$ (``the output equals the intended function''), then
$\neg R$ is the single polynomial $t_1g_1+\dots+t_kg_k-1$. The selector's $R$ is a disjunction, $z=x\vee z=y$, so $\neg R$ is
$z\neq x\wedge z\neq y$: again one polynomial, $t\,(z-x)(z-y)-1$.

\sect{3. The test runs one way}
\[
1\in I\ \Longrightarrow\ V(I)=\varnothing\ \Longrightarrow\ C\wedge\neg R\ \text{has no solution in }\F_p\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{sound}.
\]
The first arrow is immediate: evaluate $1=\sum_i h_if_i$ at a common zero to get $1=0$. The weak Nullstellensatz reverses it over
the algebraic closure $\overline{\F}_p$ only, because $V(I)$ may have points that are not $\F_p$-rational. The smallest example is
$x^2+1\in\F_3[x]$: no root in $\F_3$, yet $\langle x^2+1\rangle$ is a proper ideal, so $1\notin I$. Hence \textbf{$1\notin I$ does not
mean ``bug''}, and there are two ways to close the gap.
\par\textbf{Search} for an $\F_p$-point of $V(I)$: cvc5's model-finding phase (\S4).
\par\textbf{Add the field equations} $v^p-v$ for every variable $v$: then $V$ contains only $\F_p$-points, and $1\in I+\langle v^p-v\rangle$
holds exactly when there is no $\F_p$ solution. In the example, $1=(x^2+1)^2-x\,(x^3-x)$ over $\F_3$. At $p\approx2^{254}$ these
generators have astronomical degree, which is why solvers search instead.
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}\toprule
Logic / solver & Commutative algebra\\\midrule
conjunction of constraints & ideal generated by the polynomials\\
\textsc{unsat} over $\overline{\F}_p$ & $1\in I$ (weak Nullstellensatz: $V(I)=\varnothing\iff1\in I$)\\
\textsc{unsat} over $\F_p$ exactly & $1\in I+\langle v^p-v\rangle$ (field equations cut $V$ down to $\F_p^{\,n}$)\\
\textsc{sat} with model & an $\F_p$-point of $V(I)$; the model is its coordinates\\
unsat core & which generators occur in the certificate $1=\sum_i h_if_i$\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
\textbf{Every conclusion is about the encoded model.} A certificate proves that this $I$ has no $\F_p$-point. That $I$ is the
circuit the prover actually runs, and that the encoded $R$ is the intended one, sit outside the algebra.

\sect{4. What cvc5 actually computes (no sampling anywhere)}
\textbf{Refutation.} It computes a Gr\"obner basis of $I$ (Buchberger/F4) for a monomial order and reduces $1$. The reduced basis is
$\{1\}$ iff $1\in I$, and the computation produces an explicit \textbf{Nullstellensatz certificate} $1=\sum_i h_i f_i$: a finite
identity that is a proof of emptiness for all $p^n$ candidate points at once. If the $h_i$ have integer coefficients the identity holds
in every commutative ring, which is why the same \textsc{unsat} is returned over $\F_{11}$ and over the 254-bit BN254 field in the same
few milliseconds.
\par\textbf{Model finding.} If the basis is not $\{1\}$, $V(I)\neq\varnothing$ over $\overline{\F}_p$ but an $\F_p$-point still has to be
found. Zero-dimensional case: a lexicographic basis is triangular, so solve the univariate element by exact root finding in $\F_p$
($\gcd$ with $x^p-x$, Cantor--Zassenhaus) and back-substitute. Positive-dimensional case: fix a free variable, recompute, recurse,
backtrack. Each step is exact elimination; the returned model is re-checked against every constraint.
\par\textbf{Three answers.} cvc5 omits the field equations of \S3. So \textsc{unsat} is a proof (even over $\overline{\F}_p$),
\textsc{sat} comes with a checkable witness, and a third answer, \textsc{unknown}, exists when the point search gives up or times out.
A fuzzer can only ever say \textsc{sat}; it has no object corresponding to the certificate.
\par\textbf{How big the basis gets.} For $n$ variables of degree $d$, Gr\"obner basis degrees are bounded by $d^{2^{O(n)}}$ (Dub\'e), and
ideal membership needs doubly exponential degrees in the worst case (Mayr--Meyer). With finitely many solutions over $\overline{\F}_p$
the bounds drop to single exponential, $d^{O(n)}$. Over $\F_p$ there is one relief: coefficients never grow, since each stays a
$\log_2p$-bit field element, so the blow-up is only in the number and degree of terms. Structure matters more than size: circomlib's
\texttt{Num2Bits} at $b=32$ did not finish in a week (\S8). Every practical method shrinks what one basis is computed over: split
bases (\S9), one gadget at a time, or one slice of a circuit at a time.

\sect{5. Worked example: the selector}
Constraint $f_1=z-sx-(1-s)y$ and repair $f_2=s(s-1)$ (slide 21). The negated specification $z\notin\{x,y\}$ is the single
polynomial $g=t\,(z-x)(z-y)-1$ of \S2. Reducing modulo $f_1$,
\[
(z-x)(z-y)\;\equiv\;(1-s)(y-x)\cdot s(x-y)\;=\;s(s-1)(x-y)^2 \pmod{f_1},
\]
and $f_2$ kills the right-hand side. Writing $h=z+sx+(1-s)y-x-y$ for the cofactor of $f_1$, the identity (verified by expansion)
\[
\boxed{\;1\;=\;t\,\bigl[h\,f_1+(x-y)^2 f_2\bigr]\;-\;g\;}
\]
is the certificate: $1\in I$, so the repaired selector is sound in every field. With two Rabinowitsch variables,
$g_1=t_1(z-x)-1$ and $g_2=t_2(z-y)-1$, it reads $1=t_1t_2[h\,f_1+(x-y)^2f_2]-(g_1g_2+g_1+g_2)$. Delete $f_2$ and
$\langle f_1,g\rangle$ has the $\F_{11}$-point $(x,y,z,s)=(3,7,5,6)$ with $t=(2\cdot(-2))^{-1}=8$: cvc5 answers \textsc{sat} and
prints it. The Lean theorem \texttt{selector\_sound} is the same argument in two cases via \texttt{mul\_eq\_zero}.
\par\textbf{Where the field axiom enters.} Over $\mathbb Z/6$, $s=3$ satisfies $s(s-1)=0$ and gives $z=3x+4y=4\notin\{x,y\}$ for
$(x,y)=(0,1)$, yet the certificate is a valid ring identity. No contradiction: $(z-x)(z-y)=4\cdot3=0$ in $\mathbb Z/6$, so $g=-1$
and the encoding reports ``$\neg R$ fails'' although $z\notin\{x,y\}$. Both readings in \S2, ``$\neq0$'' as ``is a unit'' and
``$\wedge$'' as ``the product is nonzero'', hold exactly in a field; that coincidence is what ``sound over every field'' rests on.

\sect{6. Public and private wires play different roles}
The variables of $I$ are not interchangeable. The verifier fixes the public $x$: it \emph{is} the claim. The prover chooses $\wb$,
possibly adversarially. A free private value is harmless if it cannot reach $x$, like the hint of slide~18; a public value not tied to
the computation is the bug, as in Example~0. The labels decide which ideal answers which question.
\par\textbf{Statement level: eliminate the private wires.} With a lexicographic order putting $\wb$ above $x$, a Gr\"obner basis of
$\langle C\rangle+\langle v^p-v\rangle$ contains a basis of the elimination ideal
$I_x=\bigl(\langle C\rangle+\langle v^p-v\rangle\bigr)\cap\F_p[x]$. Its zeros are exactly the public statements the circuit can
prove. For the circuit of slide~9 over $\F_{11}$:
\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}\toprule
Constraints & $I_x\subset\F_{11}[x_0]$ & public values accepted\\\midrule
$C_0,C_1,C_2$ (Example 0: $C_3$ dropped) & $\langle x_0^{11}-x_0\rangle$ & all of $\F_{11}$, including the false $7$\\
$C_0,\dots,C_3$ & $\langle(x_0-3)(x_0-5)(x_0-6)(x_0-8)(x_0-9)(x_0-10)\rangle$ & $\{3,5,6,8,9,10\}=\{w_0^4+5\}$\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}
Statement-level soundness is the containment $V_{\F_p}(I_x)\subseteq\{x : \exists w\ R(w,x)\}$.
\par\textbf{Instance level: substitute the public values.} Plug numbers in for $x$; only private wires remain variables, and the question
is whether \emph{this} claim has a witness. Pinning the selector to $(3,7,5)$ is this case.
\par\textbf{Determinism: duplicate the private side.} Tools like Picus share the inputs $x$, copy every other wire, and ask whether the
outputs can differ: $C(\wb,x,y)=0\wedge C(\wb',x,y')=0\wedge\sum_i t_i(y_i-y'_i)-1=0$, the disjunction row of \S2. This is the
query split Gr\"obner bases attack (\S9).
\par Elimination needs the field equations to see only $\F_p$-points, so at $p\approx2^{254}$ it is the conceptual picture; solvers work
instance by instance or with the determinism query.

\sect{7. cvc5 versus Zirgen: different layers of the stack}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.47\textwidth}p{0.5\textwidth}@{}}
\textbf{cvc5} is a general SMT solver (Stanford/Iowa). Its finite-field theory
\texttt{QF\_FF} (Ozdemir, Kremer, Tinelli, Barrett, CAV~2023; split Gr\"obner bases, CAV~2024) decides polynomial systems over
$\F_p$ as in \S4. Input: constraints in SMT-LIB. Output: \textsc{sat}+model, \textsc{unsat}, or \textsc{unknown}. It
\emph{analyses} circuits and is the backend of Picus; it never produces one. &
\textbf{Zirgen} is RISC~Zero's circuit DSL and MLIR-based compiler. It \emph{produces} circuits: AIR constraints plus witness
generation, emitted as Rust/C++/CUDA/Metal prover and verifier code for the RISC Zero zkVM. It decides nothing. Verification
reaches it from outside: Nethermind's Lean model of Zirgen's MLIR with a verification-condition generator, and Veridise's
zirgen-to-LLZK frontend, through which Picus (hence cvc5) and zkLean become available.
\end{tabular}

In the language of this note: Zirgen is where the ideal $I$ gets written down; cvc5 is one of the procedures that decides whether
$I$ has a rational point. Compiler versus decision procedure, not competitors.

\sect{8. The bitsum wall}
Take the simplest range check there is, for $p\ge 2^b$:
\[
\textstyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{b} X_i(1-X_i)=0,\qquad X_1+2X_2+\dots+2^{b-1}X_b=0,\qquad X_bZ=1 .
\]
A human refutes it in one line: the bits are Boolean, so the bitsum forces every $X_i=0$, contradicting $X_bZ=1$.
A full Gr\"obner basis for this system takes time \emph{exponential in $b$}. The 2023 cvc5 solver could not decide determinism of
circomlib's \texttt{Num2Bits} at $b=32$ ``even in a week''. The diagnosis (their \S3.2) is sharp: the bitsum alone is already a
Gr\"obner basis, the bit constraints alone are already a Gr\"obner basis, and it is \emph{their combination} that blows up.
Translating to bit-vectors is a dead end because the surrounding equations are genuinely field equations. And the pattern is not
exotic: 98\% of public Circom projects use bitsums, and 64\% of the variables in circomlib's SHA-2 sit inside one. This is slide 24
of the workshop at industrial scale.

\sect{9. The fix: several small bases plus one non-algebraic fact}
A \textbf{split Gr\"obner basis} for $I$ is a tuple $(B_1,\dots,B_k)$ of Gr\"obner bases with $\langle B_1\cup\dots\cup
B_k\rangle=I$. Each $B_i$ answers ideal membership for its own subsystem only; in exchange it may be cheap. The procedure
\textsc{Split} refines the tuple by passing polynomials between bases, and a hook \textsc{extraProp} may inject any polynomial
provably in $I$ by \emph{other} means. The concrete instance \textsc{BitSplit} uses $k=2$: a \emph{sparse} basis holding everything
except bitsums, and a \emph{linear} basis holding all linear polynomials including bitsums, so that a bitsum and its bit constraints
never meet in one Buchberger run. Its single propagation rule is \textbf{congruence of binary representation}: if $Y-\sum 2^{i-1}X_i$
and $Y'-\sum 2^{i-1}X'_i$ are present, all bits are known Boolean, $m<\log_2|\F|$, and $Y-Y'\in I$, then every $X_i-X'_i\in I$.
For \texttt{Num2Bits} determinism that is the whole proof: with $f=X'_j-X_j$ propagated and $g=(X'_j-X_j)Z-1$ the disequation,
$1=fZ-g\in I$. Soundness and termination are proved (their Thm.~3), and the search phase is complete in principle. Numbers at a
300\,s limit over 1140 benchmarks:
\begin{center}\small\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}\toprule
& all solved & \texttt{QED2} (100) & CirC determinism (640) & CirC soundness (100)\\\midrule
Monolithic (2023) & 475 & 13 & 191 & 38\\
\textsc{BitSplit} (2024) & 969 & 100 & 582 & 59\\
\textsc{BitSplit} without the bitsum propagator & 344 & 0 & 131 & 34\\
\bottomrule\end{tabular}\end{center}

\sect{10. What it does not fix}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{The worst case is unchanged.} ``In the worst case, \textsc{BitSplit} builds a GB for the full system''; complexity stays
doubly exponential. The gain is heuristic, and the third row above shows it is almost entirely the propagator.
\item \textbf{The propagator matches a syntactic pattern.} It fires only on $Y-\sum 2^{i-1}X_i$ with $m<\log_2|\F|$ and bits already
known Boolean. Signed-digit or base-$2^k$ limb decompositions, bitsums rewritten by earlier algebra, and any decomposition with
$2^m>p$ (the slide-24 pitfall, where representation is not unique) receive no help and fall back to raw Gr\"obner computation.
\item \textbf{Integer semantics are out of reach.} The authors' own account of what remains: ``\textsc{BitSplit} struggles only with
division and remainder; verifying their determinism would require understanding that integer division is deterministic, as encoded
in field constraints.'' That is precisely the quotient-and-range argument of slides 25--26 and of foreign-field gadgets like fake GLV.
\item \textbf{Determinism is not soundness.} The breakthrough is on determinism queries, $\Phi(X,Y)\wedge\Phi(X,Y')\wedge Y\ne Y'$,
whose specification writes itself. Queries against a real specification $R$ (CirC soundness, 59/100) remain hard, and the
selector of \S5 is sound for a reason determinism alone cannot express.
\item \textbf{One query per bitwidth and per field.} Each benchmark fixes $b$ and $p$. Nothing is learned about $b+1$.
\item \textbf{Incomplete internal propagation and thin CDCL(T) integration}, both acknowledged: linear-to-sparse propagation is
incomplete (their Example 1, and completing it did not help), and the solver ``constructs no theory lemmas''.
\end{enumerate}

\sect{11. When to rely on a Lean proof instead}
A Lean proof is not a search, so it has no \textsc{unknown}: a human supplies the argument and the kernel checks it, and the
cost is human time rather than solver time. That cost is worth paying exactly where the list above says the solver stops.
\textbf{Parametric statements.} ``Binary representation is unique whenever $2^b\le p$'' is one theorem for all $b$ and all $p$,
a short induction; the solver needs one query per $(b,p)$ and times out past $b\approx32$ for the hard operators.
\textbf{Integer semantics.} The argument ``$xy-z-3k\in\{-5,\dots,4\}$ and the only multiple of 11 there is 0'' is a lift from
$\F_p$ to $\mathbb Z$ followed by linear arithmetic; in Lean that is \texttt{omega} after \texttt{ZMod.val} bookkeeping, and it
scales to 254-bit moduli and multi-limb representations unchanged. This is the division-and-remainder gap, closed.
\textbf{Specifications, not just determinism.} \texttt{selector\_sound} and \texttt{scalar\_cancellation} in the repository state
$C\Rightarrow R$ against the intended relation, over an arbitrary field and an arbitrary module, which no determinism query can.
\textbf{Group-theoretic hypotheses.} Prime-order subgroup membership and $v\not\equiv0 \pmod{\ell}$ are hypotheses about a
module over $\F_\ell$, not polynomial identities in $\F_r$; they are natural in Lean and have no SMT encoding of practical size.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Working rule.} Run the push-button tool first: \textsc{sat} is an exploit, \textsc{unsat} closes a fixed-size gadget.
Escalate to Lean when the answer is \textsc{unknown}, when the property is parametric in $b$ or $p$, when the proof needs
integers or groups rather than field identities, or when the property is soundness against a specification rather than
determinism. The caveat of \S3 still applies: the theorem is about the model, so the circuit-to-model step (LLZK, zkLean) is where
the remaining trust sits.
